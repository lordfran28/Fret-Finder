# Playing the bass on the guitar

Our notes, in our words, from the owner's books. Page numbers are the books' own. Nothing here is copied from the
books' music; the examples are ours.

## What a bass line is for (Friedland, *Building Walking Bass Lines*, Hal Leonard 1993)

- A walking line is "one step after the other that takes you somewhere": every note is going to the next chord (p. 4).
  The process is always the same: look at the chords, choose the notes, choose their order (p. 4).
- Keeping time comes first; a line that doesn't land on the beat is worse than no line (p. 6).
- **Targets and approaches** (pp. 18–31). The note on the first beat of a chord is the *target*: at first the root,
  later the 3rd or 5th (p. 41). The note just before it is the *approach*. Four kinds:
  - chromatic: a half step above or below the target (p. 18); twice in a row it pulls harder: two half steps from
    above or below (the double chromatic approach, p. 21);
  - dominant: the target's 5th, above or a 4th below (p. 22);
  - scale: the scale note next to the target, above or below (p. 27). When the chords move round the circle of
    fifths, the 5th of the first chord is already the upper scale approach to the next (p. 27);
  - indirect: two approaches round the target, one from each side: lower chromatic then upper scale, upper scale then
    lower chromatic, lower scale then upper chromatic, lower then upper chromatic, upper then lower chromatic
    (pp. 48–49). They are strong enough to make an odd note sound right.
- A note that is a chord tone and an approach at once makes the line stronger (p. 23).
- **Timing a run** (p. 38): count the notes between where you are and the target. A scale walk that has four notes
  for four beats arrives on time; one that arrives early needs an approach note to fill the gap; one that is long
  needs a leap (an octave) to start it in the right place. When two chords move by a 5th, walking down the scale from
  the first root lands on the next root on beat 1 (p. 39).
- **Arpeggios** (pp. 43–44): spell the chord, but the last note before a change is always an approach. Going from a
  minor 7 chord to a dominant a 4th higher, the minor chord's 7th is a half step under the dominant's 3rd (p. 44).
- **Two chords as one** (pp. 45–46): a ii–V can be walked as the ii alone, or as the V with the ii's root on top
  (a sus4), which frees the line from root after root.
- **Chromatic motion** (p. 51): a line moving by half steps carries itself; keep it balanced with notes in the chord.
- **Two-feel and four-feel** (p. 13): roots (and 5ths) in half notes for a ballad's head; quarter notes for solos.
- **Behind a singer** the plain, obvious line is often right; the freer line suits a horn solo (p. 46).
- His order for a new tune (p. 54): roots, then 5ths, then approaches, then scale lines through the changes, then
  arpeggios and other targets on beat 1, then chromatic motion and indirect resolutions.

## On the guitar: bass and chords together (Tracy Kim, *Walking Bass Study #2*, I–vi–ii–V in C)

- The chord, three or four notes with its bass on the 6th or 5th string, on beats 1 and 3; on beats 2 and 4 a single
  bass note that approaches the next chord's bass by a half step from below, from above, or mixed (pp. 1–2).
- The chord can come on the "and" with the bass on the beat (pp. 5–6).
- The bass line itself suggests chords: diminished chords make the bass climb by half steps (C, C♯, D, D♯);
  tritone substitutes make it fall by half steps (E, E♭, D, D♭); secondary dominants and approach chords a half step
  above or below the next chord (pp. 3–4, 9–10).
- The same works high on the neck: bass on the 6th string at the 8th–12th fret and the A string at the 10th–13th,
  three-note grips on the D, G and B strings above (pp. 7–10). The bass does not have to be low to be a bass; it has
  to be under the chord and moving.

## Bordoneos: short runs on the bass strings (Graciano, *Método de guitarra tango*, ch. 2, pp. 21–25)

- A bordoneo is a phrase on the 6th, 5th or 4th string that decorates the accompaniment (p. 21).
- Into the next chord's bass on the last beat: two sixteenths a half step at a time from a whole tone below or above;
  three from a minor 3rd below; four from a major 3rd below, often one finger sliding (pp. 21–22).
- Whole phrases: to the dominant, a turn round the tonic (down a half step and back) then up the scale with the last
  step a half step; to the tonic, from the dominant's root up to its 3rd, down a step, then down the scale (p. 23).
- To end a piece: from the dominant's 9th down the scale to the leading note, then tonic, dominant, tonic (p. 23).
- A pick-up into a section: the 5th below on the "and", then four sixteenths climbing by half steps (p. 24).
- Inside one chord, on a weak beat: the bass climbs from the root to the 3rd (or falls to the 7th) in two sixteenths
  and an eighth, and the chord sounds again over the new bass (pp. 24–25). Not to a major 7th, he warns.
- His walking bass with chords (ch. 6, pp. 51–55): the chord's rhythm over the walking bass (held, twice, once
  short, on the beat), the bass formulas by kind of chord, and the advice to use it a bar or two at a time.

## Under a singer (Yupanqui, *La guitarra y el canto*, transcr. Morales)

- Under the voice the bass falls by steps, each step with its own chord: "Los Ejes de mi Carreta", bars 19–22,
  D minor, D minor over C, B half-diminished, B♭, A. In the gaps and interludes the guitar breaks the chord over a
  held bass.

## What our good pages already do

- *Cloudy*, bars 5 and 10: on beat 4, while he breathes, four quick notes on the D or low E string into the next
  chord's bass. *Я вас любил* (G), bars 25 and 34: the top held, the bass sinking a half step a beat. *How Deep Is the
  Ocean*, bars 1–4: the record's bass walking down a fret a bar under held top notes. *I Wonder*, bars 16 and 19:
  octaves climbing B♭, C, D into the high E♭, and the record's C, B♭, A♭.

## Our rules (from the above, for every arrangement)

1. Know the bass line first: the root of every chord on its beat. That alone is the frame.
2. Where the voice holds or rests before a change, the bass goes to the next chord: one approach note on the beat
   before (half step, 5th, or scale step), or a run of three to five quick notes timed to land on beat 1
   (count the notes; fill with an approach, start with a leap if it is too long). Every note in the run is either a
   chord tone or on its way to one.
3. Under the voice, the bass moves only by step or not at all: root and 5th, an inversion that puts the next chord's
   bass a step away, a line that falls or climbs a step per chord. Contrary to the tune where it can be.
4. Not every bar. A run is worth hearing because the bars around it are plain.
5. The high grips stay; the bass moves on the strings under them, at the same position when it can (bass on the 6th
   string at the 8th–12th fret, the A string at the 10th–13th), and the hand moves only where the run takes it.
6. Every run is a card that names its source and its notes, and shows as numbered dots.
