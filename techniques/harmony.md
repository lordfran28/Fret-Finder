# Varying a song's chords (Diego Sola)

Our notes, in our words, from the owner's copy of Diego Sola's *Recursos armónicos para guitarra: para componer o
variar la base armónica de tus canciones* (Melos, Buenos Aires 2019). Page numbers are the book's own. The book's
examples are Argentine songs (a rock song by Los Fabulosos Cadillacs, Yupanqui's zamba *Piedra y camino*); nothing
of them is copied here. The Exercises entry *Varying a song's chords* (`studies/sola/`) sets these ideas on the
owner's songs.

## What each chord does (p. 9)

- Three jobs: **rest** (the tonic, I), **tension** that resolves to rest (the dominant, V), and **half-tension**, a
  step on the way to tension (the subdominant, IV).
- The other chords share notes with those three and can stand in for them: II does the subdominant's job, VII the
  dominant's, VI can take the tonic's place, and III is tonic or dominant depending on what's around it.
- In minor, when the V chord is major (E or E7 in A minor), that one chord comes from the harmonic minor scale,
  because a dominant is major (p. 16).

## Moving from chord to chord (pp. 12–16)

- Every major chord has five places on the neck and every minor chord three (pp. 13–14). Sola names them by the open
  chord each looks like; on our pages they are described plainly ("barre at the 5th fret, root on the 6th string").
- A progression stays in one region of the neck: when the first chord is played up at the 7th fret, the next chords
  are taken from the grips around the 7th fret too, so no note has to travel far (p. 15). A small table on p. 15
  pairs each major place with the minor place that sits next to it.
- **The top notes move by step; the low notes may leap**, because the top is what the ear follows (p. 16).

## Colour notes (pp. 17–25)

- Sevenths, ninths, sixths and elevenths colour a chord without changing its job, and on a dominant can sharpen it
  (p. 17).
- They are not always wanted: some music asks for the plain chord, and in some styles colours sound wrong (p. 17).
- **Never against the tune**: when the melody sounds E, no chord may hold an F, an E♭ or a D♯ (p. 17). A colour note a
  half step from the sung note is wrong.
- Grids of the colour chords in every region: major 7, 9, dominant 7, 7♭9, minor 7 and minor 9 (pp. 18–22); minor
  7/11, minor 6, major 6, half-diminished, diminished, diminished 7, and the dominant colours 9, ♯9, 13, ♭13 and ♭5
  (pp. 23–24). These are linked by keeping the top notes close, and only the fretted strings sound.
- **Which colour suits which chord**, from the table on p. 25, in a major key:
  - I: major 7, 9, 6
  - II: minor 7, 9, minor 7/11, 6
  - III: minor 7, minor 7/11
  - IV: major 7, 9, 6
  - V: 7, 9, 13, and the sharper 7♭9, 7♯9 and 7♭13
  - VI: minor 7, 9, minor 7/11
  - VII: half-diminished (with the 11th)
- In a minor key, the same table:
  - I and IV: minor 7, 9, minor 7/11 (IV also 6)
  - II: half-diminished
  - III and VI: major 7, 9, 6
  - V minor: minor 7, minor 7/11
  - V made major, and the major chord on the 7th degree (B♭7 in C minor): every dominant colour
  - The dominant's sharper colours (♭9, ♯9, ♭13) are outside the scale but allowed, because a dominant is there to
    pull.

## Doing it to a song, in five steps (pp. 26–28)

1. Take the song's chords.
2. Find the key from the chord charts (pp. 10–11).
3. Name each chord's degree and its job.
4. From the table, write two or three coloured versions of each chord.
5. Find grips for them close together on the neck.

- A V chord written without a 7th in the song takes only a 9th, a gentler pull. Only a V7 takes the sharp colours
  (p. 27).
- A chord borrowed to lead into the next one (A7 before Dm in a zamba, outside the key) is a dominant of the chord
  after it. Any chord with a 7 in its name is a dominant and takes the dominant colours (p. 27).

## Putting another chord in the dominant's place (p. 29)

- The diminished chord a half step below the tonic (C♯ diminished, or diminished 7, for A7 in D).
- The dominant with a flattened 5th (A7♭5).
- The dominant a half step above the tonic (E♭7, with a 13th or ♭13th, for A7 in D). Its bass slides down a half
  step into the tonic.
- Each changes what the bass does, so the guitar's lowest note must not clash with the bass (p. 29).

## Adding dominants (p. 30)

- Treat each chord as a home for a moment and put its own dominant in front of it, as a passing chord.
- The diminished 7th a half step below the chord it leads to gives the softest sound. D, Bm, G, A becomes
  D, A♯dim7, Bm, F♯dim7, G and so on.
- Watch the bass and the tune. Sometimes a plain major or minor chord is all that's needed (p. 30).
- In 3/4 a bar can hold the dominant and then its substitute, or three chords with the same job, one a beat (p. 30).
- The last pages build a song in A from four plain chords and then colour it four ways (p. 31).

## Our rules (from the above and the owner's own)

1. The song's real chords come first. A colour or a new chord goes in only for a reason the ear accepts: the voice
   holds or rests, a phrase ends, a repeat wants a change, or the record does it. And only where it still sounds
   like the song.
2. Colours come from the table above and never sit a half step from the note he sings.
3. Plain where plain is right. Not every chord gets a 7th or a 9th, and no run of 6ths and 9ths for their own sake
   (his rule). A V written without a 7th gets a 9th at most.
4. A progression stays in one region of the neck, mostly the closed grips around the 5th–6th fret, with the top
   notes moving by step. This is the same rule as his "closed barre shapes around the 5th to 6th fret".
5. A passing dominant, most often the diminished 7th a half step below the next chord, goes where a bass moving by
   step wants it: the end of a phrase, a gap, a turnaround. Not in front of every chord.
6. A substitute dominant changes the bass. He plays alone, so the guitar's bass is the bass, and the new bass line
   must still walk: the dominant a half step above the tonic falls by half step into it (see rule 2 in
   `techniques/bass.md`), and the diminished chord on the 7th degree rises by half step into it.
7. Where the record has its own bass, the guitar's lowest note agrees with it.
8. A reharmonization that is good but not clearly better goes in the compendium (chord options, bar by bar), with
   the book page and what the tune note is in the new chord. It goes into the arrangement only when it is clearly
   better, or when he says yes.
