# Octaves and the ballad chord melody (Wes Montgomery)

Our notes, in our words, from the owner's copy of *Wes Montgomery Guitar Folio: Improvisations and Interpretations*,
transcribed and annotated by Steve Khan, edited by Phil Groeber (revised edition, 1994). Page numbers are the book's
own. The folio is transcriptions with a little text, not a method, so most of what follows is read from the music.
The Exercises entry *Octaves* (`studies/octaves/`) sets these ideas on the owner's songs.

## How a Wes solo builds (p. 4)

- Khan describes the usual order of a long Wes solo: single lines first, then octaves dropped in as punctuation, then
  whole choruses of octaves, then chords dropped into those, and last a few choruses of chords.
- *West Coast Blues* is the folio's one complete example: four choruses of single lines, three of octaves, two of
  chords (from p. 7).

## Octaves as Wes plays them (pp. 10–13, *West Coast Blues*; p. 21)

- His octaves begin as short answers: one octave on the beat, rests, then octaves off the beat (p. 10).
- Then lines: octave eighth notes running through the changes, sliding by half steps into chord notes, with rests
  between phrases (pp. 10–11).
- Riffs: one octave repeated in a rhythm, with accents and slurs, against the changing chords (p. 10, chorus 6).
- The shapes: on the 6th and 4th strings or the 5th and 3rd, the high note is two frets above the low one; on the
  4th and 2nd or the 3rd and 1st, three frets above. The string between is muted. The folio gives no strings; these
  are the standard shapes. He played them with his thumb, which is well known but not in the folio.
- A unison: the same note on two strings at once, usually the G and B, written with a + in the folio (p. 21).

## Ballads in chords (Section II, pp. 42–51)

- Five ballads played as chord melody (pp. 42–51). Two of them, *I've Grown Accustomed to Her Face* and *While
  We're Young*, are played with no band at all, which is the owner's case (p. 42).
- Khan's notes on this section (p. 42): Wes brings something simple, personal and romantic to a standard; some
  harmonies sound modern; and he uses open-string passages most jazz players of his time didn't.
- What *I've Grown Accustomed to Her Face* shows (pp. 48–49):
  - An intro over the open low E held as a pedal: E major, then F major over E, E again, B7♭9 over E, A minor 7 sus
    over E, ending on F♯ major 7 (♯4) under a fermata. The F major over E is the Spanish colour Khan mentions.
  - The tune is the top note of a chord on its strong notes, with single notes or triplets in between.
  - The bass is held from the first beat of the bar.
  - At a phrase end, the chords move under one held tune note: A7 sus, A7 13, A7 ♭9.
  - Fermatas let it breathe. It is played slowly (quarter note = 69) and freely.

## Our rules (from the above and the owner's own)

1. An octave passage is a line, not a chord. It goes in the gaps, the intro, an interlude or the instrumental, and
   states the tune or the record's horn or string line. Never under the words: an octave line fights the voice.
2. Build the way Wes builds. Within a song, single notes come before octaves, and octaves before chords; a song
   doesn't open at its biggest.
3. Octaves start as answers, an octave or two with rests between, before they run as lines.
4. Octave shapes stay locked and slide. They sit in the region of the song's grips, mostly between the 5th and 10th
   frets. Every octave passage is shown as numbered dots, like a run (CLAUDE.md).
5. He plays alone, so the bass keeps going under octaves, on beats 1 and 3 at least. On the low pairs of strings the
   octave's low note can carry it; on the high pairs, use an open string or a bass note between phrases.
6. Intros, interludes and endings he plays alone can be chord melody in Wes's way: the tune on top, a chord on each
   strong note, single notes between, the bass held from the first beat, rubato and a fermata at the end of each
   phrase.
7. Where he holds a sung note, the chord can change colour under it (sus, 13, ♭9), as Wes does under his held tune
   notes. But no colour may sit a half step from the note he holds (`techniques/harmony.md`, rule 2).
8. In keys where the tonic or the 5th is an open string (E, A, D), an intro can hold that open string as a pedal and
   move chords over it, the major chord a half step up giving the Spanish colour. This pairs with Graciano's ♭III, III,
   ♭II over an open-string pedal (`techniques/fills.md`).
9. The unison on two strings thickens a held note in an intro or ending. Use it sparingly.
