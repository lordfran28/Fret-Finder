# The guitar and the song in Argentine folk (Atahualpa Yupanqui)

Our notes, in our words, from the owner's copy of *La guitarra y el canto de Atahualpa Yupanqui* (Atahualpa
Yupanqui and Jorge Morales, Melos de Ricordi Americana, Buenos Aires, 1st ed. 2007). Page numbers are the book's own.
The book has no text about playing. It is eleven songs (zambas, chacareras, an Andean song, a milonga), each set
for one guitar under the voice, so everything below is read from the music. The lyrics and tunes are under
copyright, and nothing of them is copied here. The Exercises entry on Argentine folk (`studies/folk/`) sets these
ideas on the owner's songs.

It is the owner's case exactly: one singer, one guitar.

## How each song is laid out

- **The guitar has its say first.** Every song opens with an intro of four to twelve bars that the guitar plays
  alone, its own tune or the song's, and it comes back between verses (*Instrumental*, p. 8) or for the dance's
  stamping section in a chacarera (*zapateo*, p. 16).
- **Under the voice, the dance.** Once he sings, the guitar mostly plays the dance's own strum, the zamba strum
  (*rasgueo de zamba*, pp. 12, 22, 28) or the chacarera strum (*rasgueo de chacarera*, p. 16), with full chords held
  for the bar. In the Andean song and the milonga it is a bass note on the beat and a short chord off it (pp. 8, 18).
- **Where the voice holds or rests, a short answer** on the top strings, often a quick rising arpeggio of the next
  chord in sixteenths, landing on its first beat (pp. 8, 12, 22).

## The intros: two-note shapes moving along the neck

This is the "half triads" sound.
- **The tune in thirds and sixths.** The intro's tune is played on the top strings with a second note a third or a
  sixth under it: two notes of the chord, not all three. The pair moves step by step along the scale and up and
  down the neck (*Camino del Indio*, p. 8; *Zamba del grillo*, p. 28).
- **Over a held bass.** Under the moving pairs, a low bass note rings for one or two bars, often an open string
  (pp. 8, 22, 28).
- **Fast repeated pairs.** In the milonga the pairs come in sixteenths, each one struck twice, sliding by step and
  half step: a shimmering line in thirds and sixths (*Los ejes de mi carreta*, p. 18).
- **One figure on each chord.** In *Piedra y camino* (p. 22) one short figure is played on each chord in turn: a
  falling run in pairs, then a rising arpeggio back up in sixteenths.
- **The rising arpeggio as a link.** A run up through the next chord in sixteenths, ending on its downbeat, ties the
  phrases together (pp. 12, 16, 22, 28).

## Where the hand sits

- Many songs are set in a barre position, marked at the top: barre at the 2nd fret (p. 16), the 5th (p. 18), the 7th
  (p. 12). The intro's pairs are played inside that position.
- Songs in D and D minor drop the 6th string to D (*6ta en Re*, pp. 22, 28) so the open low D can ring under
  everything.

## Under the singing, a moving bass

- Under a falling line in the song, the bass walks down by step with the chords broken over it: D minor, D minor
  over C, B half-diminished, B♭, A (*Los ejes de mi carreta*, p. 18).
- In the Andean song the bass on the beat steps between the root and the 5th (p. 8).

## Our rules (from the above and the owner's own)

1. The guitar has its say in the intro and between verses. An intro of four to eight bars in thirds and sixths, the
   song's tune or a phrase of it, over a held bass.
2. The pairs are two notes of the chord a third or a sixth apart, moving by step along the scale on the top strings,
   up and down the neck. This is his "half triads". Show them as numbered dots, like a run.
3. Keep them inside the song's barre position, as the book does. For us that is mostly the closed grips around the
   5th–6th fret, his own rule.
4. Under the voice, the song's own rhythm, plain and full: the strum for a zamba or chacarera, a bass and an off-beat
   chord for a song or a milonga. Not a busy figure under the words.
5. Where he holds or rests, one short answer: a rising arpeggio into the next chord, or two or three pairs. Not every
   gap.
6. Where the song falls by step, the bass falls with it under the singing (`techniques/bass.md`).
7. In D or D minor, drop the 6th string to D when it lets the low D ring under the intro.
8. Outside folk songs, the pairs in thirds and sixths still suit an intro or an interlude, as long as the song's own
   style takes them. A zamba strum does not belong in a standard.
