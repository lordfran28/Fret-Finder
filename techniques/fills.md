# Fills, cadences and turns for the singer's gaps (Julián Graciano)

Our notes, in our words, from the owner's copy of Julián Graciano's *Método completo de guitarra tango* (Melos,
Buenos Aires 2016). Page numbers are the book's own. The bordoneos (runs on the bass strings, ch. 2) and the walking
bass with chords (ch. 6) are in `techniques/bass.md`. The Exercises entries on tango (`studies/tango-*`) set these
ideas on the owner's songs.

The book is written for a guitarist accompanying a singer, which is the owner's case: one guitar, one voice.

## The guitar alone with a singer (ch. 8, pp. 83–86; ch. 9, p. 87)

- With a singer, the guitar prepares an introduction and an interlude between the verse and the chorus, where it
  can show itself (p. 83).
- As a whole orchestra, in three layers (pp. 84–86):
  - The tune an octave up, on the top three strings, mostly the 1st and 2nd.
  - The tune with only a bass under it: root, 3rd and 5th, the 2nd as a passing note, the 7th on the dominant,
    which falls to the 3rd of the next chord. Bass in half and quarter notes.
  - The tune with dressed-up chords: inversions, a chromatic chord leading in, the tune in two or three voices
    (thirds or sixths under it), the turns below.
- The art is in mixing the three. An intro or interlude uses few of them; a whole piece played alone can use them
  all (p. 86).
- When two guitars accompany a singer, one keeps the chords in the middle and low strings and plays the bordoneos.
  The other fills the singer's silences on the top three strings or draws chords higher up (p. 87). Playing alone,
  the owner is both: the first guitar under the words, the second in the silences.

## Fills (ch. 3, pp. 27–33)

- **What a fill is** (p. 27): a phrase placed where the tune rests on a held note, or where there is silence. Fills
  are played on the top three strings; the bordoneos are on the bass strings. A bordoneo taken up one or two octaves
  becomes a fill.
- **Scale fills** (pp. 27–28): a quick run down (or up) the scale over the dominant, landing on the next chord, in
  major and minor.
- **Pattern fills** (p. 28): the scale in a repeating interval pattern.
- **Little bells, campanitas** (p. 28): one note of the chord struck in rising octaves across the strings, ringing
  like bells, into the next chord. From Carlos Di Sarli's piano; on the guitar, Ubaldo De Lío.
- **Tenths** (p. 29): a note and the note a tenth above it, climbing by step into the chord, in plain eighths or
  with a sixteenth flick. Played on the 6th and 3rd strings, the 5th and 2nd, or the 4th and 1st. From Francisco
  De Caro's left hand in the 1920s.
- **Octaves** (p. 30): a scale line whose notes jump between two octaves, from the tonic to the dominant or back,
  ending on a muted stroke.
- **Sixths inside a held chord** (pp. 30–31): a sixth a half step away slides onto the chord's root, 3rd or 5th,
  for major, dominant and minor chords.
- **Thirds** the same way (pp. 31–32), onto the 3rd, the 5th, or the 7th of a dominant. Change between a chord
  filled with thirds and the next with sixths.
- **Four strokes** (pp. 32–33): four short chords on the beats, each a different inversion of the dominant on the
  top four strings, into the tonic. Pianists do this with octaves; on the guitar it is done with inversions.

## Cadences: the link between sections and the ending (ch. 4, pp. 35–38)

- A link between sections goes in the 4th bar of a phrase, set for each pair of keys: minor to minor, minor to
  major, major to minor (pp. 35–36).
- ♭III, III, ♭II over the tonic held in the bass. Best in keys where an open string can be that bass (p. 36).
- The ending goes in the 3rd bar of the last phrase (p. 37). Its kinds:
  - Diminished chords moving by half steps, bass on the 6th string (p. 37).
  - On the 5th string, V7, then the diminished chord on ♭VI, then the dominant of V with its 5th in the bass, then
    V with its 3rd in the bass, so the leading note is in the bass (p. 38).
  - De Caro's line: over the dominant, a chromatic line rising from the 5th of the scale to the tonic (C, C♯, D, D♯,
    E, F in F). It comes from the old guard of tango (p. 38).
  - The double line: De Caro's line on top while the bass falls from the dominant's root, two lines against each
    other. In minor, the 3rd the bass passes through becomes the minor 3rd (p. 38). See
    `techniques/counterpoint.md`.
  - De Caro's line in octaves (p. 38).

## Turns: moving a still chord (ch. 5, pp. 41–49)

- Under a held minor tonic, chords stepping up the scale (Im, IIm7♭5, ♭III), then the dominant of IV into IVm
  (p. 41).
- A held Im given a falling bass: the minor 7th in the bass, then the dominant of V (or a passing diminished), then
  ♭VI7 (p. 42).
- A relative of the Andalusian cadence through ♭VII6 and ♭VImaj7 to V7, with a held note on top (p. 42).
- A cycle of fifths from IVm at twice the speed of the chords (pp. 42–43), or one that leads into the relative major
  for half the phrase and comes back to minor (p. 43).
- The tritone substitute (pp. 43–44): either in place of the V7, or after it in the same bar, never before it, since
  going there and back loses the pull. Often with a ♯11.
- The diminished chord on the dominant's 3rd (B dim for G7) in its place, moved every minor third. The four strokes
  can be four different diminished chords, watching that the top note resolves well (p. 44).
- The cliché line on a minor chord (Im, IVm, or IIm in major), falling from the octave or rising from the 5th, in the
  low voice on the 5th or 4th string or the middle voice on the 4th string, mostly broken into notes (pp. 44–47).
- The key's tritone (its 4th and 7th degrees) swapped for the tritone a minor third away, which gives four
  resolutions into the tonic. Played on the 4th and 3rd strings, or the 4th and 2nd (pp. 47–49). It works for
  secondary dominants too (p. 49).

## Our rules (from the above and the owner's own)

1. Fills go only where he holds a note or rests, never over the words. That is his rule (less under the voice, more
   in the gaps) and Graciano's definition (p. 27).
2. Fills sit on the top three strings, above his baritone, and the bass keeps going under them (`techniques/bass.md`).
3. Every fill leads into the next chord, onto one of its notes. The card says which chord it sits over and which
   it leads into.
4. Where the record's own instrument fills the gap, the guitar plays that (ARRANGING.md, step 3). The kinds above are
   for gaps the record leaves empty, or for the intro and interlude.
5. Not every gap, and not the same kind twice running. Thirds and sixths take turns (p. 31).
6. Each song gets an intro, and where the form has room an interlude between verse and chorus, made from its own tune
   in the three layers (p. 83). That is where the guitar can say most.
7. A link between sections goes in the 4th bar of a phrase; an ending goes in the 3rd bar of the last phrase.
8. The turns are reharmonizations, so they follow `techniques/harmony.md` (only where it still sounds like the
   song; the compendium unless clearly better).
9. The tango marks (four strokes, the little bells, the tenths) belong to tangos, milongas and valses. Elsewhere,
   only where they suit the song.
