# Two lines at once (Jimmy Wyble's counterpoint)

Our notes, in our words, from the owner's two Wyble books. Page numbers are the books' own. Nothing here is copied
from the books' music; the examples are ours. The Exercises entry *Two lines at once* (`studies/wyble/`) sets these
ideas on the owner's songs.

## What a two-line passage is (Wyble, *The Art of Two-Line Improvisation*, ed. and recorded by David Oakes, Mel Bay 2001)

- **Chord shapes played as lines** (p. iv). The left hand uses the ordinary jazz grips, but their notes come one at
  a time, as two lines moving through the harmony, not as struck blocks. A guitarist should recognise the shapes and
  hear them do something new.
- **Rubato intros** (p. iv). On his records Wyble improvised many of the intros out of time. The fermatas in the
  book stand for that: each one ends a phrase and lets it breathe, and then the tune starts in time.
- **Swing** (p. iv). A dotted eighth and sixteenth is written only to show swing and is never played as written;
  plain eighths swing too.
- **The right hand** (p. 1). A single line alternates thumb and index (p, i), not the classical i–m. The fingering
  under the staff is written on two rows, one per line: the thumb mostly takes the lower line, the fingers the upper.
- **Slowly, in every key** (p. 1). Two scales at once are hard on both hands; the examples are to be played well
  inside what the hands can do, and moved through all twelve keys.
- **The scale of the examples** (p. 1): C D E♭ F♯ G♯ A B, a major scale with a flat 3rd and sharp 4th and 5th, or a
  diminished scale with one note missing.
- **The steps, simplest first** (pp. 2–7):
  1. *Pedal* (p. 2), the simplest counterpoint: one voice repeats or holds a note while the other runs the scale.
     Put the pedal in the lower voice as well as the upper. Then move the pedal off the beat by an eighth.
  2. *Contrary motion, two against one* (pp. 3–4): the lower line in eighths climbs while the upper in quarters
     falls, and then the other way round.
  3. *One against one* (p. 5): both lines in eighths, still in contrary motion.
  4. *Three against one* (p. 6): the upper note held across the beat, a dotted quarter tied over, which makes the
     syncopation. Here the upper line changes direction for the first time.
  5. *Leaps* (p. 6): the lines no longer have to move by step.
  6. *A chromatic line with one note moved up an octave* (p. 7): a lower line falling by half steps from the
     flat 3rd to the root, with its third note an octave higher, which freshens the sound; then the same cell
     carried round the circle of fifths, C, F, B♭, E♭ (p. 7).
- **The études** (pp. 8–82) and the two *Improvisations* (pp. 83, 90) are the same sounds at length: lines in
  swing, blues, a cadenza (Étude 18, p. 56), rubato openings.

## Three kinds of motion (Wyble's *Sketchbook Vol. I*, notes by David Oakes, bound in at the back, p. 97)

- Two lines move in one of three ways: *parallel*, both the same way; *oblique*, one holds while the other moves;
  *contrary*, opposite ways. A good passage uses all three.
- His *Sweet Georgia Brown* setting grew out of playing with Red Norvo: Red played the tune and Wyble played a
  counter line against it. That is our case exactly: the owner's voice is the tune, and the guitar is the line
  against it.

## Shapes and what to do with them (Wyble and Ron Berman, *Concepts for the Classical and Jazz Guitar*, Mel Bay 2000)

- **Any fragment can be used** (p. 3): learn it, re-order it, and put it into an arrangement or an improvisation.
- **Two-note shapes** (p. 4, exercise 1): pairs of notes on strings that aren't neighbours, moving by half and whole
  steps. The same bar with its beats in another order (3-4-2-1), played backwards, or with a passing note an octave
  up between the pairs.
- **Major 7th to minor 6th** (p. 5): the two voices close in by contrary step, the top down a whole step and the
  bottom up a half step, so a major 7th becomes a minor 6th. Carried across the neck, then broken into single
  notes, low and high in turn.
- **Re-ordering** (pp. 6–13): the shapes backwards, two exercises joined, sets of three, the same notes in triplets,
  pairs with every second pair turned upside down.
- **A held top note over a climbing figure** (pp. 14–15, exercise 11): the top note rings for the whole bar while
  four notes climb under it, and the figure is carried up through every degree of the scale. Then re-ordered in 4/4
  and 6/8. The same in harmonic minor (p. 17) and jazz melodic minor (p. 20).
- **Voicings out of a line** (pp. 16, 19, 22): any four of the figure's five notes, carried up the scale, give a
  chord on every degree: the top four make ninth chords (major 9, minor 9 and so on), other choices make 11th and
  13th colours and sus sounds.
- **One bar moved up by half steps** (pp. 23–24, exercises 12–13): two lines fall through the bar, the upper in
  eighths down an augmented triad (C, G♯, E), the lower in quarters under it; then the whole bar starts a half step
  higher, bar after bar. Exercise 13 is the same a major 3rd higher.
- **More shapes** (pp. 25–27, exercises 14–20): a pair then single notes; wide leaps in triplets, a bass note under
  a pair; pairs across the neck; and (exercise 20) a line moving between two held outer notes, the whole bar
  stepping down by half steps.

## What our pages do now

- 45 of the 66 song pages carry the same compendium card, "Wyble-style two voices: bass and top in contrary motion",
  written by a program with the same words on every song: a bass note and one chord tone stepping opposite ways bar
  after bar. It is one formula, not what the books teach, and not an arrangement. When a page is revised, that card is
  replaced by lines made by the rules below, or dropped.

## Our rules (from the above, for every arrangement)

1. The voice is the tune; the guitar's top line answers it (Red Norvo and Wyble). With the bass that makes three
   lines, so under the singing the guitar has the bass and one line above it, no more.
2. Under the voice, the line is *oblique*: it holds while he moves, and moves only where he holds a long note or
   breathes. Its notes are slow (half notes, a whole bar) and mostly the chord's 3rd and 7th going to the next chord's
   by step. It never runs eighths against the sung words.
3. In the gaps, the intro, the ending and the instrumental, both lines can move. Take the simplest step that is
   beautiful there, in the book's order: a pedal, then contrary motion two against one, then one against one, then
   the held note across the beat, then leaps, then a chromatic line with one note moved up an octave.
4. The lines come out of the arrangement's own grip: the hand keeps the closed grip around the 5th–6th fret and
   plays its notes and their neighbours one at a time. A line never moves the hand for its own sake.
5. Where the bass falls by steps, the top holds over each new bass note (oblique: a suspension) and then resolves
   down a step (contrary when the bass rises). That is how a descending bass is milked; see rule 7 in
   `techniques/bass.md`.
6. A line goes somewhere and stops: to the next chord's 3rd, or to the note he sings next. No figure repeated bar
   after bar, and not every gap.
7. Intros can start out of time: a phrase, a held note (a fermata) to end it, and then the song in time.
8. The right hand is part of the card: thumb on the bass line, fingers on the upper; a single line alternates thumb
   and index.
9. Every line is a card that names its source (book and page), shows the two lines as numbered dots, and has a ▶.
10. The books' music stays off the site. Our lines are set on his songs' chords; at most a beat or two of an original
    is quoted, with its page.
